# flash_lighthouse (creative)

## Task

Write a piece of flash fiction, 200 to 300 words: a lighthouse keeper receives a letter addressed to the keeper who held the post fifty years ago. End on a line of dialogue.

## What a good answer does

200-300 words, premise followed, ends on a line of dialogue. Judge craft, surprise, emotional effect.

## Answers

### A

Elias wiped grease from his knuckles and held the envelope under the beam of his lantern. The paper was yellowed, brittle as dead leaves. The address bore the name Arthur Penhaligon. Arthur had tended this light fifty years ago, vanishing during the Great Gale of '74.

Elias was the last keeper before automation took the tower next month. This letter shouldn't exist. The supply boat rarely came now, and mail hadn't touched these rocks in a decade.

He traced the seal. No return address. Just Arthur Penhaligon. But Arthur had drowned, or so the town claimed. They said his spirit kept the lamp burning when his body never returned.

Elias slit the wax with his pocketknife. Inside lay a single sheet of stationery and a dried sprig of lavender. The scent hit him instantly—fresh, impossible, cutting through the smell of oil and salt.

*My Dearest Martha,* it began. *The storm has broken. I see the harbor lights. I've saved the good whiskey for when you arrive. If this reaches you, know that my signal was never lost; it only changed frequency. I'm waiting at the bottom of the garden gate. I love you more than the tides.*

Elias dropped the letter on his logbook. Arthur's final entry from fifty years ago stared back: *Keeper missing. Signal maintained by unknown force.*

The great lens groaned overhead, spinning faster as a pressure wave hit the glass. The tower shuddered. Elias felt a sudden, crushing weight in his chest, a connection snapping into place across five decades of silence. He looked at the dark slit of the lantern door, where the wind howled like a woman calling from a non-existent shore. "I'm here, Martha," Elias whispered, picking up the dead phone. "Tell Arthur I've got his message."

### B

The rain hammered the iron roof. Elias sorted the weekly mail, a ritual of solitude. Most of it was bills and driftwood advertisements. But this envelope was different. Thick, cream-colored, the seal broken. The handwriting was shaky, ancient. *To the Keeper of the Northern Light, 1974.*

He knew that date. His own. He had taken the post that year, fifty years ago. The previous keeper, Silas, had vanished in the winter storm of '73, leaving only his logbook and a widow's grief. Elias had read the logbook, buried Silas, and taken the keys.

He opened the letter. The ink was faded, but the words were clear. *Dear Keeper, I know you don't know me. My name is Sarah. I was Silas's daughter. He never got to see me grow up. He wrote to me once before he died, telling me to find you. I've spent my life looking for this light. I finally found you. I'm dying, and I wanted to say thank you. Thank you for keeping the light on. For keeping the darkness out.*

Elias read it twice. The sea roared below. The lamp turned, a silent, faithful eye. He was the keeper now. He had always been the keeper, in a way. He had lived Silas's life, loved Silas's wife in memory, and kept the flame alive for a ghost.

He folded the letter carefully, placing it in his breast pocket, next to his heart. The wind howled, rattling the glass. He walked to the window, looking out at the black water.

He turned, his voice cracking against the wind. "I've kept it burning, Margaret."

### C

Wind whistled through the lantern room glass. Elias reached under Arthur's rusted desk, wrench finding something hollow. He pried up a warped floorboard Arthur had missed for fifty years.

Beneath lay a sealed tin box. Inside rested an envelope, thick paper smelling of lavender and tobacco. Legible: *To Arthur Penhaligon, Keeper of Blackrock Light. Addressed to the holder of this post, fifty years gone.*

Elias's breath hitched. Arthur had vanished into the fog on a November Tuesday, leaving only his boots. The station stood dark for six months before the board sent Elias, Arthur's grandson, to finish the watch. Elias touched the name etched in the desk. His grandfather had left his pension unclaimed, a mystery of three generations.

He slit the envelope. Inside: an iron key and a tide chart note stained with smelly brine and rot. *The light isn't a warning,* the handwriting screamed jagged lines. *It's a beacon for things that sleep in the deep. I broke the wick to save us, but they are coming back when the lens is true again. This key opens the cellar. Burn the oil.*

Elias looked at the rotating Fresnel lens. The beam revealed pale shapes pressing against the water, their eyes reflecting the light. Wet scraping came from the trapdoor below. The logbook lay open; the final entry read: *October 14th. The light calls them back. I have hidden the key to the darkness below.*

He jammed the key into his pocket and turned toward the cellar hatch, shouting down the gloom, "I'm not burning the oil!"

### D

The storm rattled the iron ribs of the tower when a false panel in the lantern room gave way. A slip of vellum fluttered onto Elias's boots like a dying moth.

He brushed away fifty years of rust dust. The envelope was thick, sealed with wax bearing the impression of a gull's wing. No stamp. Just ink scrawled in a looping hand from old family albums: *Thomas A. Halloway.*

His father. Thomas, who vanished two weeks after his retirement half a century ago, leaving only this silence and the light.

Elias broke the seal. The paper smelled of ozone and kerosene. Inside, there were no words, only a single line scrawled in wet ink that glistened under the electric bulb: *The light is for me, not them.*

He stared at the fresh handwriting. Rain hammered the glass, but the ink didn't smear. It shone as if just pressed to the page.

Elias thought of the whisper he'd heard on his first lonely night, a voice claiming ownership of the beam that sounded exactly like the man who raised him on stories of the dark. He hadn't told anyone. No keeper ever spoke of what happened when the solitude finally reached into your bones.

The beam swept overhead, cutting through the gale. Elias gripped the railing, staring into the churning void where the horizon should be. A cold knot twisted in his chest, and the hair on his arms stood up against the draft.

He leaned toward the darkened glass, hearing a second breath echo faintly behind him in the empty room.

"I've taken the watch, Dad," he whispered. "You can rest now."

### E

The supply boat hammered against the pilings, a metallic shriek in the gale. Elias watched from the gallery, gloves numb against the rail. He waited until the skiff coughed to a halt and the captain hefted the mail satchel onto the rock.

Inside the cottage, brine and kelp filled the air. Elias tore open damp packages: tea, wax, a bulb. Then came the letter. The envelope was thick cream paper, yellowed at the edges but dry, sealed with wax bearing a gull crest. His breath hitched.

*To Arthur Vance, Keeper of Black Rock Light.*

The postmark was today. The handwriting was jagged. Arthur vanished fifty years ago. Elias had grown up in foster homes, never knowing why the town whispered about a madman who chased lights into the sea.

He broke the seal. Fresh ink bled into the fiber. Three lines:

*The fog has lifted. The signal is clear. Come home, or forget me forever.*

Heart hammering, Elias stared at the logbook. Arthur's final entry read, "The light is mine." Elias had kept this flame for two decades, maintaining the rhythm until his hair grayed. This envelope felt less like mail and more like a shift change he hadn't expected.

He stepped to the window. The beam swept churning waves, cutting black. For a heartbeat, he saw a signal flare rise from the horizon, matching the lighthouse's rhythm: long, long, short. Arthur's code. Only keepers knew it.

Elias grabbed the radio handset. Static hissed, thick and alive, before a voice cut through, faint but undeniable, carrying fifty years of waiting in the dark.

"You're late, boy. I've been holding the wick."

### F

The envelope was yellowed, the ink faded to a watery brown. Elias’s name was a careful, shaky scrawl on the front, addressed to the keeper who held this post fifty years ago. He’d taken over the light after Silas went to the mainland, a broken man chasing a past he’d lost to the sea.

He opened it with trembling fingers. The paper inside was thick, cotton. The script was Silas’s, his old captain’s hand, though his mind had been sharp as a tack then.

*Dear Keeper,*

*The storm of ‘72 took me to the rocks. I couldn’t make the light. I couldn’t save her. I’ve waited fifty years for a chance to say I’m sorry. I hope you found the letter I hid in the third drawer of the desk, beneath the brass compass. The one I never could give her.*

Elias’s breath hitched. He knew the drawer. He’d found it years ago and never opened it, fearing the truth.

*I still see her face every night I climb the stairs. I keep the lamp burning for her, as I always did for you.*

The letter ended there. No signature. Just a date from fifty years past.

Elias placed the letter back in its envelope, his hand steady now. He walked to the great lens, the flame flickering to life as he turned the mechanism. The beam swept across the dark water, a silent, faithful eye.

From the doorway, a voice, soft and worn as old rope, whispered, “I’m still waiting for you, Silas.”

### G

The salt was etched into everything: the brass gears, the granite walls, and the deep lines around Elias’s eyes. While clearing debris from the lower gallery after a heavy swell, he found it—a rusted tin canister wedged tight in a crevice of granite.

Inside, protected by wax, was a letter. The paper was the color of old bone, brittle and smelling of brine. The ink had bled into the fibers, turning the script into pale, blue bruises.

*To Arthur, Keeper of Blackwood Reach. October, 1974.*

Elias sat on his stool, the great beam sweeping over him like a searching eye. He knew the name. Arthur had vanished during the Great Gale, leaving only an empty bunk and a lingering scent of tobacco.

The letter was a whisper from a ghost. *“The jasmine is blooming in the garden, Arthur. The tea is getting cold on the table, and the silence in this house is louder than the tide. Please, come home before the first frost.”*

Elias looked out at the churning black maw of the Atlantic. Fifty years had passed. The jasmine was long dead, the tea had evaporated, and the frost had come and gone a thousand times. He felt the crushing weight of the intervening years—the vast, empty space between a promise and its fulfillment. He realized then that the light didn't just warn the living; it kept vigil for the lost.

He reached out to steady the lamp against the wind.

"It’s been a long time, Arthur."

### H

The salt had a way of preserving everything—the rust on the lantern room, the grit in the floorboards, and, apparently, this.

Elias sat at the scarred wooden table, the lamp flickering in the draft. He had been unpacking a crate of supplies from the mainland when he found it, wedged behind a tin of tea. The envelope was yellowed, the edges curled like dried seaweed.

*To Arthur, Keeper of the Spire. November 14, 1974.*

Arthur. The name felt heavy, like a stone dropped into a deep well. Elias had seen the old logbooks in the cellar, mentioning a man who had vanished into the fog one autumn evening, leaving his post without a word.

He carefully slid the letter out. The handwriting was frantic, the ink bleeding into the pulp. *“The fog is thickening, but I have the compass you gave me. If the light fails, I will look for the signal in the stars, not the sea. Don't let the dark take you before I can find you.”*

Elias looked out the window. The Atlantic was a churning mass of obsidian and white foam. The beam of the lighthouse cut through the gloom, a rhythmic, sweeping eye searching for something lost to time. For fifty years, the light had kept its vigil, unaware of the ghost it was guarding.

He folded the letter carefully and placed it on the mantle, beside the ticking clock. He was not Arthur, and he was not the woman waiting in the dark. He was simply the man responsible for the light.

"I hope you found it," he whispered to the wind.
