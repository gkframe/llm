# dialogue_boat (creative)

## Task

Write a dialogue (no narration beyond brief stage directions) between a 16-year-old and her grandfather, arguing about whether to sell the family's old sailing boat. The grandfather is seriously ill but never says so; the reader should be able to sense it. About 300 words.

## What a good answer does

Dialogue form, ~300 words, illness conveyed only by subtext (never stated). Judge subtlety, character voices.

## Answers

### A

(Maya, 16, stands in the slip, arms crossed. Grandfather sits in doorway of cabin, weathered.)

GRANDFATHER: You can sell it. I won’t stand in the way.

MAYA: You barely can stand.

GRANDFATHER: (smiles faintly) That’s wind. The other one’s weather.

MAYA: Dad says the mooring fees are bleeding us dry. And my tuition—

GRANDFATHER: I didn’t ask about tuition.

MAYA: Then why are you pushing?

GRANDFATHER: (quietly) Because you’ll regret it when you’re older. Or because I’m asking you to.

MAYA: You’re asking me to keep a boat that won’t float.

GRANDFATHER: She’s old. Not dead. There’s a difference.

MAYA: (voice cracking) Then what is it? You can’t even hold the tiller for five minutes without stopping.

GRANDFATHER: (exhales slowly) I can hold it for as long as I need.

MAYA: Gramps.

GRANDFATHER: Don’t say that like it’s a threat.

MAYA: I’m not. I’m trying to say I’m scared.

(He looks toward the water, hand trembling on the rail.)

GRANDFATHER: I remember your first sail. You were eight. Kept yelling at the seagull.

MAYA: You hit the seagull.

GRANDFATHER: I missed. You laughed so hard you fell overboard.

MAYA: You jumped in.

GRANDFATHER: I swam. You floated.

MAYA: We don’t have a boat. We have a memory and a bill.

GRANDFATHER: (softly) Memory’s all I have left that’s mine.

MAYA: Don’t.

GRANDFATHER: I’m not trying to keep it. I’m trying to keep you from throwing away the only thing I ever loved that still smells like me.

(He coughs, turns away.)

MAYA: I’m not throwing it away.

GRANDFATHER: Then don’t let them take her.

MAYA: I will. Tomorrow.

GRANDFATHER: (quiet) Tomorrow’s not promised to me.

MAYA: Then don’t you give me a reason to wait.

(He doesn’t answer. He just sits, breathing hard, staring at the sail.)

### B

[Grandpa sits in a wingback chair beside the hull. His hand trembles as it strokes the varnished gunwale. He brings a linen cloth to his mouth; a wet, rattling cough shakes his frame. He tucks the cloth away, eyes darting.]

MAYA: The buyer's at the door Tuesday, Grandpa. Sign the cancellation. You held your breath all the way up the steps this morning. Your lips turned blue.

GRANDPA: [Voice raspy.] Blue means I'm fighting the air. The rigging just squeaks. Like old joints.

MAYA: It's not rust! It's pneumonia. Or whatever the white-coats keep diagnosing. You're fading, and you're arguing over a pile of rot to pay for this house? For nurses?

GRANDPA: [Fingers grip the armrest, knuckles bleaching.] This isn't just wood. It's forty years of north winds. Your mother learned to trim the jib on her knees here. She knew her father by the smell of salt and varnish.

MAYA: Mom would want you to have the money. Not this... [She gestures at him, his thin shoulders vanishing under a heavy cardigan.] You're shivering again. The blanket is right there. Take the money. Get warm. Let her go.

GRANDPA: [His gaze drifts past Maya to an empty spot on the deck.] I promised your grandmother we'd sail to the point when the water turned green. She's still waiting in the cockpit. Can't ask her to wait forever. The tide turns, you know.

MAYA: She's been gone five years, Grandpa. And that boat is a liability. It smells like mildew and turpentine. You're not keeping her; you're just scared of what happens when the engine stops.

GRANDPA: [Another cough, weaker now, leaves him breathless. He clutches his chest briefly.] Engine? She doesn't have an engine. She has a heart. And if you sell her... who keeps my name on the registration after I'm gone? I can't sail it, Maya. My hands won't hold the tiller tomorrow. But the boat stays registered to Elias Vance until the end.

MAYA: [Frustration cracks; she steps closer.] Your name stays if you let me sell her. The buyer pays for your care, Grandpa. Real care. Not just a hull in a garage. Just say the word.

GRANDPA: [He stares at his trembling hand, then up at her. His breathing is shallow, rapid.] Triple market value? ...The varnish needs one more coat. I can't mix it alone. My grip is gone.

MAYA: I'll do it. I'll do the varnish. You cancel the sale. Please.

GRANDPA: [A faint smile touches his cracked lips, though his eyes flutter shut.] Triple? ...Then row me down to the slip on Saturday. One last time. I have to feel the weight of her in the water before they take her away. Just... just one ride. For old times' sake.

MAYA: If we sail her, we can't sell her! Grandpa—

GRANDPA: [Shakes his head weakly, voice dropping to a whisper.] Sell the wood. Keep the trip. Row me down. You hold the tiller. I'll... I'll remember the green water with you.

### C

MIA: They're coming Tuesday, Grandpa. Five thousand. Double asking.

POP: [Eyes fixed on the hull. His hand hovers over the gelcoat, fingers trembling.] Five... that's nothing against the wind in her sails. You remember Marthasville? '74?

MIA: I was five. You fell off the deck. We aren't talking about history. The hull has blisters, Pop. The engine is seized. It takes three people to haul her. Mom can't manage the yard, and you... [Softens] You can barely climb the ladder anymore.

POP: [Shifts in his chair, catches his breath sharply.] Liar. I climbed yesterday. Checked the rudder cables myself.

MIA: I saw you sitting on the porch for an hour after, Dad. You wouldn't stand up straight. Sell it. Put the money toward the house. For a rainy day.

POP: Rainy day? [A dry, wheezing chuckle.] This ain't rain. This is saltwater. She smells like your mother's laughter. You sell her, you sell the time I taught you to trim the jib until your fingers bled.

MIA: Mom wants a life without maintenance! The hull has blisters now, Grandpa. We can't even sail her. It's just wood and canvas that's drowning us. Every time we look at this dock, you stop breathing like you can catch up. You stare at her and forget to eat. This isn't my adventure anymore; it's your anchor. And it's dragging you down. Sell her, and you come back to us. Come back to the present.

POP: [Stands abruptly, sways, grips the table edge. Knuckles white.] Because if I let her go... [His gaze drifts unfocused for a beat, then snaps back; he touches his chest briefly.] ...then who checks the varnish? The boat is where I go when the walls get too quiet. If she's gone... I don't know who I am without her. You think selling her fixes the quiet?

MIA: Let me help you fill it! Please, Pop. Don't make me be the one who cuts her loose. Make it a partnership. I handle the sale, I take the blame if they lowball us. You just rest. No pain. No lifting. Just sign when you can.

POP: [Looks at his stiff hands, flexing them weakly.] Partnership? You'd hold my hand on the paper?

MIA: Yes. Bring me the good pencil. The black one. Not the cheap blue ballpoint. It fades, like everything else.

POP: [Nods, sinking back with visible relief.] Yeah. Chair's good. Tuesday... I want to be at the water's edge. Just close enough to hear the propeller wash. So I know she's still breathing.

### D

[Setting: The weathered wooden dock. The sun is setting. A heavy, salt-stained sailboat, *The Seraphina*, bobs gently in the background. ELARA (16) stands by a clipboard. ARTHUR (70s) sits in a folding chair, a heavy wool blanket over his legs despite the warmth.]

ELARA
The listing went live this morning. I checked the views; people are actually interested.

ARTHUR
(Staring at the horizon)
The hull looks clean. You haven’t let the barnacles take her yet.

ELARA
That’s because I haven't had time to scrub them! Grandpa, look at the math. The repairs, the slip fees... it’s draining everything. This money could—it could help with so much.

ARTHUR
(A short, shallow breath)
It’s not about the money.

ELARA
It’s always about the money! We need to be practical. You can’t even make it down to the pier without needing to sit down halfway. This boat is just... it's a lot of weight to carry.

ARTHUR
(His hand trembles slightly as he reaches for a glass of water)
It’s not weight. It’s an anchor. It keeps things from drifting.

ELARA
(Sighing)
You’re being sentimental. It’s just wood and canvas and old ropes. It’s a lot of work for something we don't even use anymore.

ARTHUR
(A thin, wet cough)
The wind doesn't care about 'use,' Elara. It just moves. 

ELARA
(Softening, stepping closer)
Please. If we sell her now, we can clear all the debts. Everything could be so much easier. No more worrying about the bills every month.

ARTHUR
(Looking up at her, eyes bright but weary)
Some things are worth the worry. You need to know what’s worth holding onto when the tide starts pulling.

ELARA
(Quietly)
I'm just trying to prepare you.

ARTHUR
(A small, knowing smile)
Then prepare the rigging. If I’m going to sit here, I want to see her ready to fly.

### E

[The offer sits on the folding table between them. A damp coil of mooring line rests nearby.]

“They’re offering fair market value, Grandpa. More than fair.”

“Fair doesn’t measure what’s in the keel.”

“It’s wood and varnish, Pop. The engine’s shot. The hull needs sanding down to the glass. It’ll cost ten thousand just to make her seaworthy again.”

“She wasn’t built for speed. She was built for patience.” [He pushes up from the chair, catches himself on the table edge. A wet cough racks his shoulders.]

“See? You can barely climb the companionway without a handrail anymore. Let her go. The money could fix the roof. Or… [She swallows.] …cover your care.”

“Care.” He tests the word like weak tea. “You think I want to be tucked away in a quiet room while she rots on a trailer? She’s been my hands when my own wouldn’t hold straight.”

“That’s not an argument. That’s guilt.”

“Is it?” [His knuckles whiten against the table.] “You can’t buy time, child. But you can keep a piece of it floating.”

“I’m not asking to erase her. I’m asking us to survive now.”

“Survival isn’t just keeping the lights on and the fridge stocked.” [His fingers tremble as they hover over the faded blueprints.] “It’s remembering what kept you warm when the Atlantic went black. They’ll chop her up for mulch or bolt it to a marina. Is that your legacy of me?”

“I want to leave you more than timber and rope.”

“Then leave me this one truth: that some things aren’t measured in dollars.” [His breath hitches, shallow and uneven.] “Just… tell them not to sign until the spring tide comes back. Let her feel the water one last time.”

“Grandpa—”

“Please.”

### F

MAYA: The broker called again. He says there’s a collector from Maine interested. They’d pay full asking price.

GRANDPA: (A thin, raspy cough) Maine is too far. The salt air won't do her any favors.

MAYA: It’s not about the air, Grandpa. It’s about the slip fees. And the hull. You haven't been down to the docks in months.

GRANDPA: I can see her from the porch. That’s enough.

MAYA: It’s not enough. It’s just... it’s a weight. You're carrying too much. The house, the garden, this... this massive, wooden debt.

GRANDPA: (He grips the arms of his chair, his knuckles turning white) She isn't a debt. She’s the only thing that still feels solid.

MAYA: She’s a ghost. You’re clinging to a ghost.

GRANDPA: (A long, shallow pause as he waits to catch his breath) Perhaps. But ghosts are reliable. They don't change. They don't... fade.

MAYA: (Voice cracking) You’re talking like she’s the only thing left. We need the space, Grandpa. I need... I need things to be simple.

GRANDPA: (He stares out the window at the mast swaying in the harbor) Everything gets complicated eventually. The wind picks up, the tide turns. You can't fight the current, Maya. You just have to find a place to drop anchor.

MAYA: But you can't stay anchored forever.

GRANDPA: (A small, tired smile) I’m not asking to stay. I’m just asking to stay right here. While I can.

### G

[Grandfather’s knuckles whiten around a ceramic mug. A listing brochure lies between them.]

GRANDFATHER: She’s taking on water again. Just a seep. I can patch her.

GRANDDAUGHTER: Dad says the haul-out alone is two thousand dollars. The marine survey came back, Granpa. It’s more than we’ll ever get back.

GRANDFATHER: [Wipes his mouth with the back of his hand] Two thousand. That’s nothing. She’s been in my family since before your father learned to swim. You don’t throw her away because the weather turned rough.

GRANDDAUGHTER: We’re not throwing her away. We’re selling her. And I’m not letting you spend every weekend chipping barnacles while you’re supposed to be resting at the clinic.

GRANDFATHER: [Sets the mug down carefully, too carefully] The clinic doesn’t know what it’s like when the wind catches the mainsail and you haven’t got a care in the world but how many knots you’ll pick up before dinner. You think I want to sit in that recliner? Watching paint dry? I’ve got one good window left this month, maybe two.

GRANDDAUGHTER: [Voice cracks] Then let someone else take her where they can actually sail her. Please. Just sign the paper. Let us help you carry this.

GRANDFATHER: [Stares at the brochure. His breath hitches, just once. He presses two fingers to his temple.] Help me? Or get it over with?

GRANDDAUGHTER: I’m trying to keep you here, Granpa. At home. With us. Not chasing a leaky hull out past the breakwater.

GRANDFATHER: [A long pause. He drags a trembling hand across the table, stopping inches from the paper.] You think I don’t know what’s eating me? What they’re feeding me to stop it? [He looks up, eyes bright but heavy.] She won’t forget the tides just because my hands shake. But if you sell her now… who’ll remember how to read the water when I’m gone?

GRANDDAUGHTER: [Softly] You are.

GRANDFATHER: [Closes his eyes. Nods slowly.] Then give me until Tuesday. Before the next round. Let me get her in one last time. Just… before the quiet comes for good.

### H

Grandpa: Hand me the chart. The old Atlantic. Pencil marks...
Maya: You can’t even hold the tiller steady.
Grandpa: I held it steady for forty years.
Maya: You held it while you were... (stops) Grandpa.
Grandpa: While I was learning. That’s all. (coughs once, covers mouth) Go on.
Maya: I’m not trying to erase it. I’m trying to save you.
Grandpa: I’m not asking you to save me.
Maya: You are. This boat is the only thing you think you can still control.
Grandpa: Your grandmother bought her. I named her. I rebuilt her.
Maya: You loved her.
Grandpa: I loved you both. That’s different.
Maya: No. It’s the same love. Just bigger.
(He tries to laugh, stops, breathes.)
Grandpa: Don’t be clever.
Maya: I’m being practical. The ribs are rotten. The mast needs new...
Grandpa: I know the mast.
Maya: Then let me find someone to take the tiller. Someone with working lungs.
Grandpa: I have working lungs.
Maya: Grandpa.
Grandpa: I said I did.

(He closes eyes, hand trembling on rail. Maya watches.)

Maya: I’m not selling her to get away from you. I’m selling her so you don’t have to look at her and see what you can’t do.
Grandpa: And what will you do with the money?
Maya: Pay the repair. Insurance. A hospital bill.
Grandpa: There’s no boat like that one.
Maya: There was.
Grandpa: (quietly) There was.

(He reaches for her hand; she lets him. His grip is weak.)
Grandpa: If I can’t sail her, I don’t want her to be someone else’s story.
Maya: It’s already our story.
Grandpa: She’s not a story. She’s a place.
Maya: Then come with me. Sell her together.
Grandpa: I can’t.
Maya: I know.
Grandpa: No, you don’t. You don’t know what it was like when the floorboards went up through the hull and she leaked every time we breathed.
Maya: I know I don’t. But I know you.
Grandpa: Then don’t let me be the one who knows too much.

Maya: Okay. Okay. But you’re not going to make me promise not to sell.
Grandpa: I am.
Maya: You can’t.
Grandpa: I can. (He exhales, shallow.) When I’m...
Maya: Don’t.

(He smiles faintly. It hurts him.)
Grandpa: The Atlantic. Take her out. Without me.
Maya: I won’t.
Grandpa: You will. And you’ll name her something.
Maya: No, I won’t.
Grandpa: Then I’ll name her something. When I’m gone.
Maya: Grandpa.
Grandpa: The Saltwater Heart. Too sentimental.
Maya: Too sentimental.
Grandpa: Good. Keep it.

(He looks at her, eyes wet, trying very hard to be steady.)
